Lecture 2: Conditioning to Identify Causal Effects

Advanced Observational Causal Inference

2001-09-02

Where we are going

Last time, we separated four things:

  1. the causal question;
  2. the causal estimand;
  3. the identifying assumptions; and
  4. the estimator.

Today: when can conditioning on observed variables make an observational comparison causal?

The answer is graphical: choose variables that block noncausal paths while leaving the causal paths of interest intact.

Note

Central question: When does conditioning remove bias, and when does conditioning create or increase bias?

The basic observational problem

Suppose we want the effect of \(D\) on \(Y\).

The observed comparison,

\[ E[Y\mid D=1]-E[Y\mid D=0], \]

need not equal the causal effect.

A common source of trouble is a backdoor path:

\[ D \leftarrow X \rightarrow Y. \]

Conditioning on the right \(X\) can block this path.

From selection bias to conditional identification

Under strong ignorability,

\[ (Y(1),Y(0)) \perp D \mid X, \]

and with overlap,

\[ 0 < P(D=1\mid X) < 1, \]

we can recover counterfactual means from observed conditional means.

For example,

\[ E[Y(0)\mid D=1,X] = E[Y\mid D=0,X]. \]

This is the bridge from observed comparisons to counterfactual quantities.

The difficult part is not writing this assumption. It is deciding whether it is credible.

Conditioning is a causal operation, not a mechanical one

For a variable \(V\), conditioning changes the graph.

Conceptually:

  • if \(V\) is a non-collider on a path, conditioning can block that path;
  • if \(V\) is a collider, conditioning can open that path;
  • conditioning on descendants of a collider can also open a path;
  • conditioning on descendants of treatment can block or distort causal pathways.

So the question is never simply:

“Is \(V\) related to treatment?”

It is:

“What paths does conditioning on \(V\) block, and what paths does it open or truncate?”

The backdoor criterion

A path from treatment \(D\) to outcome \(Y\) is a backdoor path if it begins with an arrow into \(D\).

A set of variables \(X\) satisfies the backdoor criterion when:

  1. it blocks every backdoor path between \(D\) and \(Y\); and
  2. it contains no descendant of \(D\) that lies on a directed path from \(D\) to \(Y\).

This provides a graphical recipe for identifying admissible adjustment sets.

Important

The objective is not to control for everything.

The objective is to block the noncausal paths without disturbing the causal paths.

A simple good-control case

Suppose:

The path

\[ D \leftarrow X \rightarrow Y \]

is noncausal.

Conditioning on \(X\) blocks that path.

If the graph is correct and the overlap condition holds, adjustment for \(X\) identifies the causal effect of \(D\) on \(Y\).

Not every good control is a traditional confounder

Consider:

A variable \(Z\) could be a cause of a mediator or otherwise lie on a backdoor path without being a direct common cause of \(D\) and \(Y\).

The key criterion is graphical: does conditioning on \(Z\) block a backdoor path without opening another one?

This is the broader sense of a “good control” emphasized by Cinelli, Forney, and Pearl.

A bad control: collider bias

Consider:

\(Z\) is a collider on

\[ D \rightarrow Z \leftarrow U \rightarrow Y. \]

Without conditioning on \(Z\), this path is closed.

Conditioning on \(Z\) opens the path and induces a noncausal association between \(D\) and \(U\).

The lesson:

A pre-treatment variable can be a bad control.

The fact that a variable occurs before treatment is not sufficient to justify conditioning on it.

Collider bias: luck versus ability

Suppose admission to a selective college depends on both ability and luck:

Suppose ability and luck are independent in the applicant population:

\[ A \perp L. \]

Now restrict attention to people who were admitted.

Among admitted students:

  • someone with relatively low ability must have been relatively lucky;
  • someone with relatively little luck must have had relatively high ability.

So:

\[ A \not\!\perp L \mid S=1. \]

Important

Conditioning on a common effect induces an association between its causes.

The collider did not transmit the association before conditioning. Conditioning opens the path.

Temporal ordering is not causal ordering

Suppose a variable \(X\) occurs before treatment \(D\).

That tells us something important: \(D\) cannot ordinarily cause \(X\).

But it does not tell us that \(X\) is safe to control for.

For example, suppose enrollment in a pre-college preparation program is driven by both ability and parental wealth:

\(X\) is unambiguously pre-treatment.

But it is also a collider:

\[ A \rightarrow X \leftarrow W. \]

Conditioning on \(X\) induces an association between ability and parental wealth, opening the path

\[ D \leftarrow A \rightarrow X \leftarrow W \rightarrow Y. \]

Important

Temporal ordering rules out some causal stories. It does not determine the adjustment set.

Ask what causes a variable, not merely whether it occurs before treatment.

M-bias

A particularly important collider structure is:

\(Z\) is pre-treatment and related to both treatment and outcome, but conditioning on it can create bias by opening

\[ D \leftarrow U_1 \rightarrow Z \leftarrow U_2 \rightarrow Y. \]

This is the classic M-bias structure.

The practical implication is important: “controls for predictors of treatment” is not a sufficient rule for selecting controls.

Neutral controls and precision

A control can be neutral for identification while still changing precision.

Outcome predictor

\(Z\) is not a confounder. Conditioning on it does not remove or create a backdoor path.

But it explains some otherwise unexplained variation in \(Y\):

\[ \operatorname{Var}(Y\mid Z)<\operatorname{Var}(Y). \]

Usually good for precision.

Treatment predictor

\(Z\) is also neutral for bias if it has no relationship with \(Y\) except through \(D\).

But conditioning on it removes variation in \(D\):

\[ \operatorname{Var}(D\mid Z)<\operatorname{Var}(D). \]

Potentially bad for precision.

Note

A useful rule of thumb:

Parents of \(Y\) that do not spoil identification can improve precision; unnecessary parents of \(D\) can reduce it.

Bias amplification: the precision intuition

Now suppose there is an unobserved confounder \(U\):

Without controlling for \(Z\), variation in \(D\) comes from both:

\[ \underbrace{Z}_{\text{unconfounded variation}} \quad+ \quad \underbrace{U}_{\text{confounded variation}} \quad+ \quad \text{other sources}. \]

When we condition on \(Z\), we remove some of the unconfounded variation in treatment.

That leaves a treatment contrast that is more heavily composed of variation related to \(U\).

So the precision problem becomes an identification problem:

\[ \boxed{\text{removing useful treatment variation can amplify existing confounding bias.}} \]

\(Z\) did not create the confounding. The path

\[ D\leftarrow U\rightarrow Y \]

was already open.

Important

Adding predictors of treatment is not automatically a strategy for reducing causal bias.

Another bad control: post-treatment variables

Suppose:

If we want the total effect of \(D\) on \(Y\), conditioning on the mediator \(M\) removes part of the causal pathway we are trying to estimate.

This is often called overcontrol or post-treatment bias.

The backdoor criterion therefore excludes descendants of treatment that lie on directed paths to the outcome.

Post-treatment variables can also create collider bias

Consider:

Conditioning on \(M\) can create an association between \(D\) and the unobserved \(U\).

So post-treatment adjustment can be problematic for two different reasons:

  1. it can block part of the causal effect;
  2. it can open a new noncausal path.

Which problem occurs depends on the causal structure.

Sample selection is a collider problem

Let \(S\) indicate being observed in the analytic sample.

If we restrict attention to \(S=1\), we are conditioning on a collider.

The restriction can induce an association between \(D\) and \(Y\) even when the corresponding relationship would not exist in the target population.

This provides a graphical way to understand classic sample-selection problems.

A useful rule: ask what conditioning does to the graph

For every proposed control \(Z\), ask:

  1. Is \(Z\) on a backdoor path?
  2. If so, does conditioning on \(Z\) block that path?
  3. Is \(Z\) a collider on another path?
  4. Does \(Z\) have descendants that are being conditioned on?
  5. Is \(Z\) downstream of treatment?
  6. What causal estimand are we trying to preserve?

The same variable can be appropriate for one estimand and inappropriate for another.

Total effects versus direct effects

Suppose:

\[ D \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y \]

and we want the total effect of \(D\) on \(Y\).

Controlling for \(M\) generally blocks part of the effect.

But a different estimand—the controlled direct effect—can make adjustment for a mediator substantively meaningful.

Therefore:

\[ \text{“Should I control for }M\text{?”} \]

cannot be answered without first specifying:

\[ \text{“Which causal effect am I trying to estimate?”} \]

Why is causal mediation harder?

Suppose:

Define:

  • \(M(d)\): the mediator value that would occur under treatment \(d\);
  • \(Y(d,m)\): the outcome if treatment were set to \(d\) and the mediator set to \(m\).

The natural indirect effect at treatment level \(d\) is

\[ NIE(d)=E\left[Y(d,M(1))-Y(d,M(0))\right]. \]

This is the average causal mediation effect (ACME) in the common notation.

The ordinary ATE compares:

\[ Y(1)\quad\text{versus}\quad Y(0). \]

The ACME instead compares, for the same units, outcomes under one fixed treatment while swapping in mediator values from two different treatment worlds.

Important

The ACME contains a cross-world counterfactual.

For example, \(Y(1,M(0))\) combines the outcome under treatment with the mediator value that would have occurred under control.

Sequential ignorability is stronger in a qualitative sense

For an ordinary treatment effect, we can obtain identification from:

\[ (Y(1),Y(0))\perp D\mid X. \]

For a natural mediation effect, we need additional conditions linking the mediator to its potential outcomes, conditional on treatment and covariates.

The difficulty is not simply “two assumptions instead of one.”

The second exchangeability condition concerns a variable created after treatment:

\[ M\text{ is post-treatment.} \]

Even if \(D\) is randomized, \(M\) generally is not.

Treatment may create new common causes of \(M\) and \(Y\), so the mediator-outcome relationship can remain confounded.

Note

Randomization solves the treatment-assignment problem. It does not automatically randomize the mediator.

Even three clean experiments do not identify the ACME

Imagine three experiments:

Experiment Randomized What it identifies
1 \(D\) Effect of \(D\) on \(M\)
2 \(D\) Total effect of \(D\) on \(Y\)
3 \(M\) Effect of manipulating \(M\) on \(Y\)

That sounds like everything we need.

But consider two people:

\(D\) moves \(M\) \(M\) moves \(Y\)
Alice ✓
Bob ✓

Across the population, we can observe both average causal relationships.

But nobody’s outcome necessarily changes through the \(D\rightarrow M\rightarrow Y\) pathway.

What is missing is the within-person alignment:

\[ \text{Who responds to }D\text{ through }M? \]

and

\[ \text{Are those same people responsive to }M? \]

Important

Randomization identifies the relevant marginal causal effects.

The natural indirect effect depends on how those potential responses line up within units.

Can a better experiment solve the mediation problem?

Suppose we independently randomize both \(D\) and \(M\):

\(M=0\) \(M=1\)
\(D=0\) \((0,0)\) \((0,1)\)
\(D=1\) \((1,0)\) \((1,1)\)

This \(2\times2\) factorial design identifies the mean potential outcome

\[ E[Y(d,m)] \]

for each experimentally set pair \((d,m)\).

That is extremely strong evidence about mechanisms. It identifies controlled direct effects, the causal effect of manipulating \(M\), and treatment-by-mediator interactions.

But the natural indirect effect asks for

\[ E\left[Y(d,M(1))-Y(d,M(0))\right], \]

which requires information about each person’s natural mediator responses

\[ M_i(1)\quad\text{and}\quad M_i(0). \]

A factorial experiment still does not reveal both values for the same person.

Important

No standard experiment nonparametrically identifies the natural indirect effect by itself.

The cross-world component still requires an additional identifying assumption.

The fundamental distinction

For an ordinary ATE,

\[ ATE=E[Y(1)-Y(0)], \]

randomization solves the fundamental missing-data problem:

other randomized units provide information about the counterfactual outcome that this unit did not reveal.

For a natural indirect effect,

\[ NIE(d)=E\left[Y(d,M(1))-Y(d,M(0))\right], \]

we need more than the marginal distributions of the potential outcomes and mediator responses.

We need a joint, within-person relationship across treatment worlds.

That relationship is not observed under any ordinary assignment scheme.

Important

Randomization can identify causal effects of interventions. It cannot, by itself, reveal every counterfactual relationship we may wish to compare.

Regression is only one way to condition

Everything we have discussed is about the identification strategy, not about OLS specifically.

The same adjustment set can be implemented through:

  • regression adjustment;
  • matching;
  • inverse-probability weighting;
  • covariate-balancing weights;
  • doubly robust estimators; or
  • other adjustment procedures.

If the adjustment set does not satisfy the identification assumptions, changing the estimator does not solve the problem.

This is the bridge to our next lecture.

Good control, bad control, neutral control

A useful classification is:

Type What happens?
Good control Blocks relevant noncausal paths without opening new ones or truncating the target causal effect
Bad control Opens a noncausal path, blocks part of the target effect, or can amplify remaining bias
Neutral control Does not change asymptotic bias; may affect precision

The classification is graphical before it is statistical.

In-class exercise: diagnose the paper’s controls

Return to Arceneaux, Gerber, and Green (2010).

The authors match on:

  • prior voting behavior;
  • age;
  • party registration;
  • sex;
  • registration timing; and
  • missingness indicators.

Before seeing the matching results, ask:

  1. What assumptions does this adjustment strategy require?
  2. What unobserved characteristics could still confound contact and turnout?
  3. Which proposed covariates are clearly pre-treatment?
  4. What would it mean for a matching analysis to have excellent balance but poor identification?

The paper is designed to make that distinction visible empirically.

What you should be able to do after today

Given a proposed causal analysis, you should be able to:

  • state the causal estimand;
  • identify the relevant backdoor paths;
  • determine whether a proposed adjustment set satisfies the backdoor criterion;
  • identify colliders and explain collider bias;
  • identify post-treatment controls and explain why they can be problematic;
  • recognize sample selection as a conditioning problem;
  • understand the intuition behind bias amplification; and
  • distinguish identification problems from estimator problems.
