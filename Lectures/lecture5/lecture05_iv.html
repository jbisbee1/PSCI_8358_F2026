Recap: where Lecture 4 left off

Lectures 1–4 assumed:

\[ (Y(1),Y(0)) \perp D \mid X, \qquad 0 < P(D=1\mid X) < 1. \]

DML’s contribution was entirely about estimation:

  • AIPW: doubly robust, targets the ATE directly
  • Neyman orthogonality + cross-fitting: flexible ML nuisance models without breaking \(\sqrt{n}\)-inference

DML never touches whether conditional ignorability is true. It only changes how well you can estimate under it.

The central lesson of Lecture 4, restated

\[ \boxed{ \text{Cross-fitted, orthogonal, ML-based estimation} \not\Rightarrow \text{conditional ignorability holds} } \]

If there is an unobserved confounder of \(D\) and \(Y\), DML returns a precise, confident, wrong number.

Today’s question is not “how do we estimate better?” It’s:

What do we do when we don’t believe we’ve measured everything that confounds \(D\) and \(Y\)?

Why not just add more controls?

Recall Lecture 4: adding covariates doesn’t rescue you from an unobserved confounder — no amount of \(X\) helps if the confounder isn’t in \(X\).

Example: returns to schooling. Ability confounds education and wages. Ability is not fully captured by any observable proxy.

Selection-on-observables is not an option here — not because the tools are bad, but because the identifying assumption is not credible.

We need identifying variation that does not require conditioning on the confounder at all.

Instrumental variables: the idea

Find a variable \(Z\) that:

  1. moves \(D\) (relevance),
  2. is “as good as randomly assigned” (independence),
  3. affects \(Y\) only through \(D\) (exclusion).

If such a \(Z\) exists, we can learn about the effect of \(D\) on \(Y\) using variation in \(D\) that is driven by \(Z\) — variation we know is not itself confounded.

This is a completely different identification argument from Lectures 1–4. Nothing here conditions our way out of confounding.

The textbook (constant-effects) IV model

\[ Y_i = \alpha + \theta D_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad D_i = \pi_0 + \pi_1 Z_i + \nu_i \]

Assumptions, textbook version:

  • \(\text{Cov}(Z_i,\varepsilon_i)=0\) (instrument exogenous)
  • \(\pi_1 \neq 0\) (relevance / first stage)
  • \(\theta\) is the same for every unit

\[ \hat\theta_{2SLS} = \frac{\text{Cov}(Z,Y)}{\text{Cov}(Z,D)} \]

This is a fine starting point. It is also silent on what “the effect of \(D\)” means when effects differ across people — exactly the problem we saw with \(\hat\theta_R\) in Lecture 4.

Why the constant-effects model isn’t enough

Just as \(\hat\theta_R\) implicitly reweighted units in Lecture 3–4, \(\hat\theta_{2SLS}\) implicitly selects which units’ effects it is even capable of learning about — once we allow heterogeneous effects.

Angrist, Imbens & Rubin (1996): reformulate IV entirely in potential outcomes, with no constant-effects assumption at all.

This is where the causal content of “the instrument identifies an effect” actually lives.

Potential-outcomes setup

Binary instrument \(Z_i \in \{0,1\}\), binary treatment \(D_i \in \{0,1\}\).

Potential treatments: \(D_i(1)\), \(D_i(0)\) — the treatment unit \(i\) would take under each value of the instrument.

Potential outcomes indexed by the instrument: \(Y_i(z,d)\) — but under exclusion, this collapses to \(Y_i(d)\).

Observed treatment and outcome:

\[ D_i = D_i(Z_i), \qquad Y_i = Y_i(D_i(Z_i)) \]

Everything downstream follows from how \(D_i(1)\) and \(D_i(0)\) relate to each other.

The four IV assumptions

  1. Independence (as-if random assignment): \[ \big(Y_i(1),Y_i(0),D_i(1),D_i(0)\big) \perp Z_i \]

  2. Exclusion: \(Z_i\) affects \(Y_i\) only through \(D_i\): \(Y_i(z,d) = Y_i(d)\) for all \(z\).

  3. Relevance (nonzero first stage): \[ E[D_i(1)] \neq E[D_i(0)] \]

  4. Monotonicity: \(D_i(1) \geq D_i(0)\) for all \(i\) (or all \(\leq\) — pick a direction and hold it).

Independence and exclusion do the work that “\(Z_i\) uncorrelated with \(\varepsilon_i\)” was hiding. Monotonicity is new — the constant-effects model didn’t need it.

Compliance types

Every unit has a type defined by \((D_i(1), D_i(0))\):

\(D_i(1)\) \(D_i(0)\) Type
1 0 Complier — takes treatment iff instrument on
0 1 Defier — takes treatment iff instrument off
1 1 Always-taker — takes treatment regardless
0 0 Never-taker — never takes treatment regardless

Types are unobserved — we never see both \(D_i(1)\) and \(D_i(0)\) for the same unit.

Monotonicity says: no defiers.

Why monotonicity matters

Without monotonicity, compliers and defiers can offset each other in a way that makes the IV estimand an uninterpretable mix — positive effects for compliers and negative effects for defiers could produce any sign.

With monotonicity (no defiers), only three types remain: compliers, always-takers, never-takers.

Substantive question, not a technical one: could anyone plausibly do the opposite of what the instrument nudges them toward? If yes for even a subset, monotonicity is suspect.

The reduced form and the first stage

Intent-to-treat effect on the outcome: \[ ITT_Y = E[Y_i \mid Z_i=1] - E[Y_i \mid Z_i=0] \]

Intent-to-treat effect on treatment (the first stage): \[ ITT_D = E[D_i \mid Z_i=1] - E[D_i \mid Z_i=0] = P(\text{complier}) \]

Both are estimable directly from data — no potential outcomes needed, just comparing groups by \(Z_i\). (Independence is what makes this comparison causal.)

The LATE theorem

Under independence, exclusion, relevance, and monotonicity:

\[ \frac{ITT_Y}{ITT_D} = E\big[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0) \mid \text{complier}\big] \;\equiv\; \text{LATE} \]

This ratio is the Wald estimator, and with covariates or a continuous instrument it’s what 2SLS computes.

In words: the IV estimand is the average treatment effect for compliers — the subpopulation whose treatment status is moved by the instrument. Not the ATE. Not the ATT.

Proof sketch: why compliers?

Decompose \(ITT_Y\) by (unobserved) type, using independence to compare \(Z=1\) vs. \(Z=0\) groups that have identical type composition:

\[ ITT_Y = \pi_c\,E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid c] + \pi_a\underbrace{E[Y(1)-Y(1)\mid a]}_{=0} + \pi_n\underbrace{E[Y(0)-Y(0)\mid n]}_{=0} \]

Always-takers get \(D=1\) under both values of \(Z\) — no contrast. Never-takers get \(D=0\) under both — no contrast. Only compliers contribute, because they are the only type whose treatment status actually changes with \(Z\).

\[ ITT_Y = \pi_c \cdot \text{LATE}, \qquad ITT_D = \pi_c \]

Divide: \(ITT_Y / ITT_D = \text{LATE}\).

“Whose effect does an instrument identify, and why?”

Because always-takers and never-takers contribute zero to both the numerator and the denominator, they drop out of the ratio entirely.

IV cannot tell you anything about the effect on always-takers or never-takers — there is no variation in \(D\) for them to exploit.

The instrument doesn’t identify “the effect of \(D\).” It identifies the effect for the specific people the instrument actually moves.

Characterizing compliers

You cannot list who the compliers are (types are unobserved), but you can describe the complier subpopulation:

  • \(\pi_c = ITT_D\) — their share is estimable
  • Abadie (2003): reweight the observed data (using \(\kappa\)-weights built from \(Z\), \(D\), and covariates) to recover the covariate distribution of compliers
  • Compare complier covariate means to the full sample — who does the instrument actually move?

Always report this. A LATE for a narrow, unrepresentative complier population is a different finding than a LATE for a broadly representative one, even with the same point estimate.

What exactly does 2SLS estimate?

With a single binary instrument, no covariates: 2SLS = Wald estimator = LATE for compliers relative to that instrument.

With covariates, multiple instruments, or a continuous instrument: 2SLS is a weighted average of complier subgroup LATEs, weights proportional to each subgroup’s contribution to the first-stage variance (same \(\text{Var}(D_i\mid X_i)\)-type weighting logic as \(\hat\theta_R\) in Lecture 4).

There is no single “the LATE” in these richer designs — there is a first-stage-variance-weighted average of many local effects, potentially over different complier populations.

Mogstad, Santos & Torgovitsky (2018): make this explicit — 2SLS with covariates does not target a policy-relevant parameter without further assumptions.

Weak instruments

If relevance barely holds (\(ITT_D \approx 0\), or \(\pi_1 \approx 0\) in the first stage):

  • \(ITT_Y / ITT_D\) divides by something close to zero — small sampling noise in the denominator is amplified
  • 2SLS point estimates are badly biased toward the (biased) OLS estimate in finite samples
  • Conventional \(t\)-tests and confidence intervals are unreliable, even with a “significant” first stage

Diagnostic: first-stage \(F\)-statistic. Rule of thumb \(F>10\) is a floor, not a guarantee — treat weak-instrument-robust inference (e.g., Anderson-Rubin) as the safer default when in doubt.

What evidence can and can’t validate IV assumptions

Relevance: directly testable — look at the first stage.

Independence: not directly testable, but supportable —

  • covariate balance across \(Z\) (like a randomized trial check)
  • placebo outcomes: things \(Z\) should have no effect on

Exclusion: not directly testable in general —

  • find a subpopulation/setting where the first stage should be (near) zero; if a reduced-form \(Z\to Y\) effect survives there, that’s evidence against exclusion

Monotonicity: substantive judgment, partially testable (next slides).

Testing implications of the LATE assumptions

A violation of the testable implications below means at least one of exclusion, independence, or monotonicity fails — it does not tell you which.

Balke & Pearl (1997), instrumental inequality (binary \(Z,D,Y\)): four inequalities must all hold, e.g.

\[ P(Y{=}0,D{=}0\mid Z{=}0) + P(Y{=}1,D{=}0\mid Z{=}1) \leq 1 \]

Intuition: each term bounds the share of a compliance type consistent with a given \(Y\) value; if IV validity holds, these can’t jointly exceed 1.

Kitagawa (2015): continuous-\(Y\) generalization via stochastic dominance; proves these are the strongest testable implications — passing can never confirm validity, only fail to refute it.

Huber & Mellace (2015): bounding the always-taker/never-taker means

Under the LATE assumptions:

\[ E(Y\mid D{=}1,Z{=}1) = \frac{\pi_a}{\pi_a+\pi_c}E(Y_1\mid a) + \frac{\pi_c}{\pi_a+\pi_c}E(Y_1\mid c), \quad E(Y_1\mid a) = E(Y\mid D{=}1,Z{=}0) \]

This implies a testable trimming inequality:

\[ E(Y\mid D{=}1,Z{=}1,Y\leq y_q) \;\leq\; E(Y\mid D{=}1,Z{=}0) \;\leq\; E(Y\mid D{=}1,Z{=}1,Y\geq y_{1-q}) \]

with \(q=\pi_a/(\pi_a+\pi_c)\). A symmetric inequality holds for never-takers. Together: four testable moment inequalities, estimable from data.

Putting the diagnostics together

None of these tests can prove IV validity — Kitagawa’s result is general. What they can do:

  1. Rule out some violations decisively (instrumental inequality fails ⇒ something is wrong)
  2. Make the complier-only interpretation concrete (Abadie reweighting)
  3. Flag weak identification before you trust a point estimate (first-stage \(F\))
  4. Force you to argue for exclusion using domain knowledge, not statistics — statistics alone cannot supply it

In-class exercise

Three IV designs, same outcome and treatment:

Design A

\(Z\): randomized encouragement to enroll in a program. Strong first stage. Balanced covariates across \(Z\). No plausible direct effect of encouragement on \(Y\).

Design B

\(Z\): distance to nearest facility. Moderate first stage. Distance correlates with local infrastructure quality (plausibly affects \(Y\) directly).

Design C

\(Z\): a policy that varies by birth-cohort cutoff. Weak first stage (\(F=4\)). Reduced form is “significant.”

For each: which assumption is most at risk, and what diagnostic would you run first?

The central lesson

An instrument doesn’t identify “the effect of treatment.” It identifies the effect for whoever the instrument actually moves — and only if independence, exclusion, and monotonicity hold for them.

\[ \boxed{ \text{Valid instrument} \;\neq\; \text{ATE} \qquad \text{Valid instrument} \;=\; \text{LATE for compliers} } \]
