From Causal Questions to Identification

Advanced Observational Causal Inference

Jim Bisbee

2026-08-26

Today’s goal

You should be able to take a substantive causal claim and:

  • define the treatment, outcome, and causal estimand;
  • distinguish the estimand from an observable statistical contrast;
  • explain why an observational comparison may be biased;
  • represent a causal argument with a directed acyclic graph (DAG);
  • identify simple causal and noncausal paths; and
  • state the conditional ignorability assumption that can justify adjustment.

The recurring question: What must be true for the comparison in the data to answer the causal question we care about?

The organizing framework

Causal inference is not an estimator

A statistical procedure only has a causal interpretation after we specify the causal quantity and justify an identification argument.

\[ \boxed{ \text{Causal question} \rightarrow \text{Estimand} \rightarrow \text{Identification} \rightarrow \text{Estimator} \rightarrow \text{Diagnostics} \rightarrow \text{Causal claim} } \]

We will use this sequence for every design in the course.

Identification is not estimation

Suppose \(D_i\in\{0,1\}\) is a treatment and \(Y_i(d)\) is the potential outcome under treatment status \(d\).

A causal estimand might be

\[ ATE = E[Y(1)-Y(0)]. \]

An observable contrast is

\[ E[Y\mid D=1]-E[Y\mid D=0]. \]

These are different objects.

Identification analysis asks: Under what assumptions can a function of the observed-data distribution recover the causal estimand?

More data isn’t the solution

Imagine observing an arbitrarily large sample.

You could learn with essentially no sampling uncertainty:

\[ E[Y\mid D=1], \qquad E[Y\mid D=0]. \]

But you still would not observe

\[ E[Y(0)\mid D=1]. \]

The missing counterfactual is an identification problem, not a small-sample problem.

Note

More observations can reduce statistical uncertainty. More of the same data cannot, by itself, repair a failed identification strategy.

Start by naming the causal quantity

Two common estimands:

\[ ATE = E[Y(1)-Y(0)] \]

and

\[ ATT = E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid D=1]. \]

The ATE asks about the average effect in the target population.

The ATT asks about the average effect among those who actually receive treatment.

Why might these differ?

Estimands come before estimators

Suppose treatment effects vary across units:

\[ \tau_i=Y_i(1)-Y_i(0). \]

Then

\[ E[\tau_i] \neq E[\tau_i\mid D_i=1] \]

in general.

A method that identifies an ATT is not automatically estimating an ATE.

A useful habit throughout the semester:

Before asking “Which estimator should I use?”, ask “Which causal quantity am I trying to learn?”

Why the raw comparison can fail

For the treated population,

\[ \begin{aligned} E[Y\mid D=1]-E[Y\mid D=0] &=E[Y(1)\mid D=1]-E[Y(0)\mid D=0]\\ &=ATT + \underbrace{E[Y(0)\mid D=1]-E[Y(0)\mid D=0]}_{\text{selection bias}}. \end{aligned} \]

The second term asks whether treated and untreated units would have had the same outcomes in the absence of treatment.

The two estimands are not equally fragile

The ATT is missing only one counterfactual:

\[ ATT = \underbrace{E[Y(1)\mid D=1]}_{\text{observed}} - \underbrace{E[Y(0)\mid D=1]}_{\text{missing}} \]

so it needs only \(\textbf{(A0)}: E[Y(0)\mid D=1]=E[Y(0)\mid D=0]\).

The ATE also needs the untreated group counterfactual \(E[Y(1)\mid D=0]\), and so a second assumption

\[ \textbf{(A1)}: \quad E[Y(1)\mid D=0]=E[Y(1)\mid D=1]. \]

Note that \(Y(1)\) never appears in (A0).

A world where the ATT survives

Let baseline outcomes be unrelated to take-up, and let only the gain drive it:

\[ \begin{aligned} Y_i(0) &= \mu+\varepsilon_i, &&\varepsilon_i \perp D_i\\ Y_i(1) &= Y_i(0)+\tau_i, &&\tau_i \not\perp D_i \end{aligned} \]

Then \(E[Y(0)\mid D=1]=\mu=E[Y(0)\mid D=0]\), so (A0) holds, and

\[ \Delta = \underbrace{(\mu + ATT)}_{E[Y\mid D=1]} - \underbrace{\mu\vphantom{(}}_{E[Y\mid D=0]} = ATT. \]

Exactly—in a world where units select into treatment entirely on their own unobserved \(\tau_i\).

Which kind of selection is it?

Selection operates through ATE ATT
Levels: \(D\) depends on \(Y(0)\) broken broken
Gains: \(D\) depends on \(Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)\) broken survives

Selection on levels violates (A0) and takes both estimands with it.

Selection on gains violates only (A1): \(E[Y(1)\mid D=0]=\mu+ATU \neq \mu+ATT\).

Important

“Biased” is not a property of an estimator. It is a property of an estimator relative to a named estimand.

A substantive example

Suppose we want the effect of receiving a get-out-the-vote phone call on turnout.

Let:

  • \(D=1\): voter actually receives the phone call;
  • \(Y=1\): voter turns out;
  • target: effect of the call among people who receive it.

A naive comparison is

\[ E[Y\mid D=1]-E[Y\mid D=0]. \]

Why might people who answer a campaign phone call differ from people who do not?

From prose to a causal model

One hypothesis:

People with greater underlying political engagement may be more likely to answer the phone and more likely to vote.

The graph makes the problem explicit:

  • \(D\rightarrow Y\) is the causal path of interest.
  • \(D\leftarrow U\rightarrow Y\) is a noncausal path.

What is a DAG?

A directed acyclic graph represents assumptions about causal relationships.

  • Nodes represent variables.
  • Arrows represent causal relationships.
  • Directed means arrows have direction.
  • Acyclic means following arrows cannot bring you back to the same node.

Warning

An arrow does not mean “these variables are correlated.” It asserts a causal relationship in the model.

A DAG is therefore a representation of assumptions—not something mechanically discovered from the dataset.

Three structures to recognize

Confounder

\(D\leftarrow X\rightarrow Y\)

Mediator

\(D\rightarrow M\rightarrow Y\)

Collider

\(D\rightarrow C\leftarrow Y\)

These structures behave differently when we condition on variables. We develop those rules next week.

Paths

A path is a sequence of connected edges, regardless of arrow direction.

Consider:

There are two paths connecting \(D\) and \(Y\):

  1. \(D\rightarrow Y\) — the causal path of interest.
  2. \(D\leftarrow X\rightarrow Y\) — a noncausal path.

The second path begins with an arrow pointing into treatment. We call it a backdoor path.

Why backdoor paths matter

If both paths are open, the observed association between \(D\) and \(Y\) can reflect:

\[ \text{causal effect} + \text{noncausal association}. \]

So a central identification problem becomes:

Can we isolate the causal path \(D\rightarrow Y\) from the noncausal paths connecting \(D\) and \(Y\)?

Next week we formalize the answer using conditioning and the backdoor criterion.

Observed confounding

Suppose political engagement itself is not measured, but we observe variables related to the selection process:

Examples might include:

  • previous voting behavior;
  • age;
  • party registration;
  • registration history.

If \(X\) captures the relevant common causes of treatment and outcome, comparisons conditional on \(X\) may recover the causal effect.

Conditional ignorability

A standard identification assumption is

\[ (Y(1),Y(0))\perp D\mid X. \]

Informally:

Once we compare units with the same \(X\), treatment assignment contains no additional information about their potential outcomes.

For an ATT, the key counterfactual condition can be expressed as

\[ E[Y(0)\mid D=1,X] = E[Y(0)\mid D=0,X]. \]

What does conditioning buy us?

Under the relevant assumptions, untreated units with covariates \(X=x\) tell us about the missing untreated potential outcomes of treated units with \(X=x\).

For the ATT:

\[ E[Y(0)\mid D=1,X=x] = E[Y\mid D=0,X=x]. \]

That gives us the missing counterfactual comparison.

Important

“Control for \(X\)” is not the substantive justification. The justification is that, conditional on \(X\), untreated observations provide valid counterfactual outcomes for treated observations.

But the assumption is doing the work

Now suppose an unobserved variable remains:

Even perfect adjustment for \(X\) does not remove

\[ D\leftarrow U\rightarrow Y. \]

This is why observational causal inference depends on substantive assumptions about the data-generating process.

A DAG is an argument

When reading a paper, ask:

  1. What causes treatment?
  2. What causes the outcome?
  3. Which causes are shared?
  4. Which variables occur after treatment?
  5. Which variables are observed?
  6. Which important variables might be unobserved?

Then draw the graph implied by the authors’ argument.

Do not begin by drawing the regression specification. Begin with the causal process.

In-class exercise

A researcher argues that college education increases political participation.

They observe:

  • parental socioeconomic status;
  • high-school achievement;
  • college attendance;
  • adult income;
  • political participation.

In small groups:

  1. Define a causal estimand and propose a DAG.
  2. Identify possible confounders and a possible mediator.
  3. Identify at least one plausible unobserved common cause.
  4. State what would have to be true for a comparison conditional on pre-treatment covariates be able to identify the effect.

Competing DAGs can imply different analyses

Consider adult income.

Model A: income is partly caused by college and subsequently affects participation.

If so, income is downstream of treatment.

But another substantive theory might locate income differently.

The correct adjustment set is therefore a causal question before it is a statistical question.

What you should be able to do after today

Given a published causal claim, you should be able to:

  • identify \(D\), \(Y\), and the target estimand;
  • distinguish the causal estimand from an observed association;
  • decompose an observational comparison into effect + selection bias;
  • translate a simple substantive theory into a DAG;
  • recognize confounders, mediators, and colliders;
  • distinguish causal from simple backdoor paths; and
  • state the conditional ignorability assumption in words and notation.
