Matching in Practice: A Worked Example

Advanced Observational Causal Inference

2001-09-09

The teaching goal

We will use one synthetic dataset to see what matching actually does.

The sequence is:

\[ \text{naive comparison} \rightarrow \text{CEM} \rightarrow \text{nearest-neighbor matching} \rightarrow \text{weighting} \rightarrow \text{balance}. \]

Because this is simulated data, we also know the true potential outcomes.

Generate the data

library(tidyverse)
library(MatchIt)
library(cobalt)

set.seed(1234)

N <- 100

dat <- tibble(
  id = 1:N,
  age = pmin(pmax(rnorm(N, 40, 10), 20), 65),
  income = pmax(rnorm(N, 50 + 0.8 * age, 20), 5)
) |>
  mutate(
    college = rbinom(
      N, 1,
      plogis(-2 + 0.04 * age)
    ),

    propensity =
      plogis(
        -5.5 +
        0.055 * age +
        0.012 * income +
        0.9 * college
      ),

    D = rbinom(N, 1, propensity),

    Y0 =
      20 +
      0.35 * age +
      0.08 * income +
      5 * college +
      0.002 * income^2 +
      rnorm(N, 0, 5),

    tau = 8,

    Y1 = Y0 + tau,

    Y = D * Y1 + (1 - D) * Y0
  )

Why not exact-match?

To exactly match on the original covariates, we would need

\[ age_i=age_j,\qquad income_i=income_j,\qquad college_i=college_j. \]

With continuous age and income, this is essentially impossible.

So instead we coarsen the continuous variables.

Create CEM strata

dat <- dat |>
  mutate(
    age_band = cut(
      age,
      breaks = c(19, 29, 39, 49, 59, 69),
      labels = FALSE
    ),
    income_band = cut(
      income,
      breaks = c(0, 50, 75, 100, 125, Inf),
      labels = FALSE

  )

A matching stratum is now defined by

\[ (age\ band,\ income\ band,\ college). \]

ATT weighting

For the ATT, inverse-propensity weighting uses

\[ w_i = \begin{cases} ~~~~~~~~ 1 & D_i=1,\\[4pt] \dfrac{e(X_i)}{1-e(X_i)} & D_i=0. \end{cases} \]

This reweights the controls to resemble the treated population.

IPW: the basic idea

Inverse probability weighting (IPW) does the same conceptual job as matching:

\[ \text{construct a comparison population} \]

But instead of pairing or dropping observations, IPW changes how much each observation counts.

Let

\[ e(X_i)=P(D_i=1\mid X_i) \]

be the propensity score.

A simple example

Suppose Alice (who was treated) has

\[ e(X_A)=0.20 \]

Treatment was unusual for someone like Alice, so her ATE weight is

\[ w_A=\frac{1}{0.20}=5 \]

Now suppose Bob (who was also treated) has

\[ e(X_B)=0.80 \]

Treatment was expected for someone like Bob, so his ATE weight is

\[ w_B=\frac{1}{0.80}=1.25 \]

Alice counts more because her observed treatment state was less likely.

The basic intuition

\[ \boxed{ \text{IPW upweights observations observed in an unlikely treatment state.} } \]

For treated units:

\[ w_i=\frac{1}{e(X_i)}. \]

For untreated units:

\[ w_i=\frac{1}{1-e(X_i)}. \]

The less likely the observed treatment status, the more informative that observation becomes for reconstructing the target population.

Why does this help with confounding?

Suppose income affects treatment:

\[ income \rightarrow D \]

and also affects the outcome:

\[ income \rightarrow Y. \]

High-income people are disproportionately represented among the treated.

The raw data contain too many high-income treated units and too few high-income control units.

IPW changes their relative contributions.

What happens to the treated group?

Suppose

\[ e(X)=0.9. \]

A treated observation gets

\[ w=\frac{1}{0.9}\approx1.11. \]

Now suppose

\[ e(X)=0.1. \]

A treated observation gets

\[ w=\frac{1}{0.1}=10. \]

Treated observations who were unlikely to be treated receive much more weight.

What happens to the control group?

For controls, the relevant probability is

\[ 1-e(X). \]

Suppose

\[ e(X)=0.9 \]

but the observation is untreated.

Then

\[ w=\frac{1}{1-0.9}=10. \]

This is a particularly informative control: someone who looks highly likely to have been treated, but happened not to be.

IPW and the ATE

For the ATE,

\[ w_i= \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{e(X_i)}, & D_i=1,\\[8pt] \dfrac{1}{1-e(X_i)}, & D_i=0. \end{cases} \]

The resulting weighted groups are intended to resemble the same target population:

\[ \boxed{ \text{weighted treated} \approx \text{weighted controls} \approx \text{full population}. } \]

The ATE estimator

A normalized IPW estimator is

\[ \widehat{ATE}_{IPW} = \frac{ \sum_i D_iY_i/e(X_i) }{ \sum_i D_i/e(X_i) } - \frac{ \sum_i(1-D_i)Y_i/[1-e(X_i)] }{ \sum_i(1-D_i)/[1-e(X_i)] }. \]

The first term estimates the population mean under treatment.

The second estimates the population mean under control.

IPW versus matching

The connection to matching is easiest to see for the ATT.

Matching gives us

\[ ATT = \sum_s \Delta(s)P(S=s\mid D=1), \]

where

\[ \Delta(s) = E[Y\mid D=1,S=s] - E[Y\mid D=0,S=s]. \]

We want the control group to have the treated distribution of covariates.

IPW accomplishes the same goal continuously, without explicitly constructing strata.

ATT-IPW

For the ATT:

\[ w_i= \begin{cases} ~~~~~~~~1, & D_i=1,\\[6pt] \dfrac{e(X_i)}{1-e(X_i)}, & D_i=0. \end{cases} \]

The treated population is already our target population, so treated observations keep weight 1.

We only need to reweight the controls.

ATT-IPW intuition

Consider three control observations:

\[ \begin{array}{c|c} e(X) & \text{ATT weight}\\ \hline 0.10 & 0.11\\ 0.50 & 1\\ 0.90 & 9 \end{array} \]

A control with \(e(X)=0.90\) looks much more like the treated population.

So it gets much more weight when we construct

\[ E[Y(0)\mid D=1]. \]

Why does the ATT weight have this form?

The control group has density proportional to

\[ f(X\mid D=0). \]

The treated group has density proportional to

\[ f(X\mid D=1). \]

By Bayes’ rule,

\[ \frac{f(X\mid D=1)}{f(X\mid D=0)} = \frac{e(X)}{1-e(X)} \cdot \frac{P(D=0)}{P(D=1)}. \]

The constant term does not matter after normalization.

Thus the ATT control weight is

\[ \boxed{ \frac{e(X)}{1-e(X)}. } \]

What has the weighting accomplished?

Before weighting:

\[ f(X\mid D=1) \neq f(X\mid D=0). \]

For the ATT, we want:

\[ f(X\mid D=1) \approx f_w(X\mid D=0). \]

The weighting procedure creates a pseudo-control population that resembles the treated population.

ATE versus ATT weighting

The target population determines the weights.

\[ \boxed{ \begin{array}{c|c|c} & \text{Treated} & \text{Controls}\\ \hline ATE & 1/e(X) & 1/[1-e(X)]\\[4pt] ATT & 1 & e(X)/[1-e(X)] \end{array} } \]

ATE:

\[ \text{both groups} \rightarrow \text{full population}. \]

ATT:

\[ \text{controls} \rightarrow \text{treated population}. \]

The overlap problem returns

Suppose

\[ e(X)=0.01 \]

Then its ATE weight is

\[ \frac{1}{0.01}=100. \]

Likewise, if a control has

\[ e(X)=0.99, \]

its ATE weight is also

\[ \frac{1}{1-0.99}=100. \]

Lack of overlap appears as enormous weights.

Matching and IPW handle lack of overlap differently

CEM says:

\[ \boxed{ \text{No comparable unit? Drop it.} } \]

Ordinary IPW says:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Comparable unit is very rare? Give it a large weight.} } \]

This is the same overlap problem appearing in two different forms.

The problem with extreme weights

Extreme weights can make the estimate:

\[ \text{high variance} \]

and can make the estimate depend heavily on a small number of observations.

This is why propensity-score overlap is not just a diagnostic issue.

It directly affects how informative the data are for the causal comparison.

Overlap weights

A different strategy is to deliberately emphasize observations in regions of common support.

For overlap weights:

\[ w_i= \begin{cases} 1-e(X_i), & D_i=1,\\[4pt] e(X_i), & D_i=0. \end{cases} \]

Units with propensity scores near 0 or 1 receive little weight.

Units near

\[ e(X)=0.5 \]

receive the most weight.

The broader picture

Matching and weighting are two ways of constructing a comparison population.

\[ \boxed{ \begin{array}{ccc} \text{Matching} &\rightarrow& \text{construct comparable units} \\[6pt] \text{IPW} &\rightarrow& \text{construct comparable populations} \\[6pt] \text{Overlap weighting} &\rightarrow& \text{focus on common support} \end{array} } \]

The identification assumption remains the same:

\[ (Y(1),Y(0))\perp D\mid X. \]

The estimator determines how we use the observed data to exploit that assumption.

The main lesson

Different estimators solve different parts of the problem.

\[ \begin{array}{c} \text{Exact/CEM} \rightarrow \text{retain comparable strata} \\[6pt] \text{Nearest neighbor} \rightarrow \text{find similar units} \\[6pt] \text{Weighting} \rightarrow \text{reconstruct a target population} \end{array} \]

But in every case, we should distinguish:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What population is the estimator targeting?} } \]

from

\[ \boxed{ \text{How well did we construct that population?} } \]

and from

\[ \boxed{ \text{How much sampling/finite-sample error remains?} } \]
